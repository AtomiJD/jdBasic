## What to Do With the Time

This book has counted minutes for more than five hundred pages. It is
fair to end with the part no program can do.

Saved time does not stay saved on its own. A free half hour at four in
the afternoon is filled by the next mail within minutes, unless it
already belongs to something. Every reader whose ledger looked good
after three months had one thing in common: they decided what the time
was for before it arrived. Mia leaves on time on Wednesdays and
Fridays. Jonas holds the one-to-one talks he used to cancel. Lena took
back her Thursday mornings for the analysis work that brought her into
controlling in the first place.

None of that is impressive, and none of it needs to be. The point of
automating a chore is not to fill the hour with more work at a higher
speed. It is to have the hour, and to choose.

Some honest limits belong here too. A recipe will not fix a job that
asks too much of one person, a team that is too small, or a manager
who measures presence instead of results. If your audit showed that
most of your week is meetings you cannot decline, the recipes give you
an hour or two, and the rest is a conversation with your manager that
no program can hold for you. The ledger is useful there as well: it
shows in plain numbers where the week goes.

If this book did its job, three things are true now. A handful of
programs do the chores you used to repeat. You can read them, change
them and switch them off. And there is a line in your ledger, every
week, that says what you did with the time. Keep the line. The
programs are only there to make room for it.
